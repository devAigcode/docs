**Knowledge base software** should begin with the questions people need answered, not with a long feature checklist. A useful application helps customers resolve routine issues independently or gives employees a dependable place to find current procedures, policies, and product context. The same basic building blocks can support both goals, but the audience, access rules, and editorial workflow should differ.

A custom approach is worth considering when a team needs documentation to fit an existing product, support process, terminology, or permission model. Instead of assuming a prebuilt documentation product is required, define the experience and operating process first. Then build a small, usable version that can be extended as content and requirements become clearer.

## Define the knowledge base users and use cases

**Start by assigning a specific job to the knowledge base before deciding what to build.** The most important early decision is whether the application primarily serves customers, internal teams, or both. These groups often ask different questions and require different levels of access.

A customer self-service portal usually focuses on setup, account tasks, troubleshooting, product usage, and policies that can be shared publicly. Its readers may arrive from a search engine, a support reply, or a link inside the product.

An internal knowledge base serves a different operational purpose. It may contain support procedures, sales enablement material, onboarding guidance, incident runbooks, decision records, or team policies. Access may need to vary by department or role, and the cost of outdated content can be higher because staff may use it to make customer-facing or operational decisions.

Before writing requirements, create a short use-case inventory. For each audience, record:

- the recurring questions they need to answer;
- the task they are trying to complete after reading;
- whether the content can be public, restricted, or visible only to selected roles;
- the content owner responsible for keeping it current.

For example, a public article titled “Reset your account access” may need a short procedure and a support escalation link. An internal version of the same topic may also explain verification steps, approved communication, and when to involve another team. They may share a topic but should not necessarily share one article.

This exercise also prevents a common mistake: treating a knowledge base as a file repository. A repository stores material; a knowledge base organizes answers around user intent. If people need to know what to do, what changed, or who owns a process, structure the content around those questions rather than around internal folder names alone.

Set a clear first-release boundary. Choose one audience, a limited set of high-value topics, and a small number of content types. Articles and categories are often enough initially. Add templates, release notes, decision records, or request forms only when a defined workflow requires them.

## Plan the core knowledge base software features

<Frame caption="A manageable knowledge base needs explicit editorial states and role-based responsibility, not just an article editor.">
  ![Workflow diagram showing articles moving from draft through review to published and archived states](/images/blog/how-to-build-knowledge-base-software-for-customers-or-internal-teams/inline-1.png)
</Frame>

**Prioritize discovery, readable articles, and controlled publishing before adding advanced capabilities.** A knowledge base builder needs to make useful content easy to locate and safe to update. The first version should support the full journey from finding an answer to maintaining it.

Begin with the reader-facing experience. At minimum, plan for a homepage or landing page, category pages, article pages, search results, and a route back to related information. Categories should express concepts users understand, such as Billing, Getting Started, Account Management, or Team Operations.

Knowledge base search deserves direct attention. Browsing works when a reader knows where a topic belongs, but search matters when they arrive with a specific phrase, error, or task. Define what the search experience should return: article titles, excerpts, categories, and perhaps matched text within an article.

Next, map the editorial workflow. Most teams need at least these states:

1. **Draft** for material that is being written or revised.
2. **In review** when another person needs to verify accuracy, clarity, or policy alignment.
3. **Published** when the article is available to its intended audience.
4. **Archived** when the material should remain recorded but no longer guide readers.

Not every team needs a formal approval process, but every published article should have an identifiable owner. Assigning ownership helps when a feature changes or a process becomes obsolete. Include a visible last-reviewed field if it supports the team’s maintenance routine, but do not treat a date alone as proof that information is accurate.

Define roles alongside the workflow. Readers may only view content; authors create and edit drafts; reviewers approve selected material; administrators manage categories, access, and publishing rules. For an internal knowledge base, permission boundaries may be central. For a public portal, the important separation may simply be between public reading and authenticated administration.

Plan the content model in plain language before selecting implementation details. An article commonly needs a title, URL slug, body, summary, category, status, author or owner, and timestamps. Categories need names, descriptions, ordering, and visibility rules. Tags can be helpful for cross-cutting topics, but they should not substitute for a coherent category structure.

**Translate the content model and workflows into a small application specification, then build only what the first release needs.** A custom application is easier to scope when requirements describe screens, actions, data, and rules rather than broad requests such as “make documentation software.”

### Prerequisites

Prepare these inputs before development begins:

- a defined primary audience and a list of initial article topics;
- a basic category structure and examples of expected article formats;
- named roles and access expectations;
- a decision on public versus authenticated reading;
- a person or team responsible for editorial decisions after launch.

Write a concise requirements brief that names the pages and actions. For example: “Visitors can browse public categories, search published articles, and read an article on mobile. Authenticated editors can create drafts, assign categories, edit content, and publish only when they have the required role.” This level of detail gives a builder or development team an implementable starting point.

## Test content discovery and administration

**Test whether people can find, trust, and update answers under realistic conditions.** A functioning article page is not enough if readers cannot locate it or editors cannot safely maintain it.

Run discovery checks using representative questions rather than only category names. Ask a tester to find an answer using the wording a customer or colleague would naturally use. Observe whether they browse, search, or abandon the attempt.

Then test the full editorial path. An author should be able to create a draft, save it, preview it, submit it for review if required, and publish it with the correct visibility. A reviewer should be able to identify what changed. An administrator should be able to manage access without unintentionally exposing restricted articles.

Use a practical test checklist:

- Confirm public and protected articles cannot be accessed through the wrong route or role. - Check that search excludes content a reader is not permitted to see. - Verify category pages, article links, and related navigation after an article is moved or archived. - Review article readability on desktop and mobile layouts.

Documentation management software also needs governance tests. Determine who can change a policy, who reviews sensitive instructions, and how teams identify material that should be reconsidered. The application can support these decisions through statuses and permissions, but it cannot make them on behalf of the organization.

## Prepare the application for ongoing improvement

**Treat launch as the start of an editorial operating process, not the end of a software project.** Once people use the knowledge base, their searches, support requests, and failed journeys will reveal gaps that planning alone cannot fully predict.

Create a simple intake path for missing or unclear content. Support agents and internal users should be able to flag an unanswered question, a confusing instruction, or an outdated article. Route these inputs to the content owner, then decide whether the right response is a new article, a revision, a product change, or clearer navigation.

Review information architecture periodically. Categories that grow too broad may need subcategories; categories with little content may be better merged. Do not reorganize solely for visual neatness. Change the structure when it improves how readers locate and understand information.

Build maintenance into normal operations. Each important article should have an owner, a review trigger, and a reason for its existence. Triggers might include a changed workflow, a product release, a recurring support question, or feedback that readers could not complete a task. This approach is more dependable than scheduling indiscriminate rewrites.

Keep extensibility in view if the application supports business-specific processes. You may later need links from support tickets, role-specific landing pages, article feedback, approval records, or connections to internal systems. A custom build can accommodate these needs when its source remains editable and its underlying content model is not overly rigid. ## FAQ

### What features should knowledge base software include?

Start with articles, categories, clear navigation, search, and a controlled way to create and publish updates. Add authentication and role-based permissions when content or editing access must be restricted. More specialized features should follow demonstrated workflow needs.

### Can a non-technical team build a knowledge base?

A non-technical team can define content, workflows, roles, and reader journeys, which are the essential inputs. A builder that generates application components from requirements may help with implementation, but the team still needs someone accountable for content accuracy, access rules, and maintenance.

### Should a knowledge base be public or internal?

Make customer-facing help public when it is safe to share and intended to support self-service. Keep operational procedures, private policies, account-specific instructions, and sensitive context inside an authenticated internal knowledge base. Some organizations need both, with separate content and permissions.

### How can I keep a knowledge base easy to maintain?

Limit the initial content types, assign each important article an owner, use a clear draft-to-publish process, and collect feedback on gaps. Review articles when underlying workflows change rather than relying only on a calendar date.

## Conclusion

**Build knowledge base software around answer-finding and accountable maintenance.** Define the audience first, create a focused content model, implement browsing and search alongside publishing controls, and test both reader discovery and editorial tasks. A smaller custom application with clear ownership is generally easier to improve than a broad portal with unclear content, permissions, and review responsibilities.
