A Reddit user named CheesecakeOdd3075 did the math in July 2026 and posted the results to r/squarespace. Their subscription had climbed 61% since they first signed up in 2021 — from $216 a year to $348. The breaking point wasn't just the number. It was what the number was paying for. "I feel like I'm paying for things I sincerely did not ask for," they wrote, "the biggest thing being AI. I don't use AI at all whatsoever in Squarespace's features because I DON'T NEED IT."

They weren't alone. Another user reported a 47.83% increase hitting renewals after August 12, 2026, calling it "a pretty wild jump, especially considering I haven't seen any major new features announced." A photographer on Fstoppers pointed out that Squarespace had raised prices 19–26% across popular tiers, explicitly citing "AI design tools most photographers don't need."

This isn't a story about Squarespace, though. It's a story about a pattern that every small business owner who subscribes to software is living through — and a question that more of them are starting to ask out loud: *Am I going to rent my business software forever?*

---

## The pricing spiral you're trapped in

Here's the structure of the trap, laid bare.

Squarespace went private in October 2024 after being acquired by Permira, a global private equity firm, in a $7.2 billion deal. Private equity acquisitions of established software platforms follow a predictable playbook: lock in the user base, reduce competitive pressure by going private, then expand margins. Price hikes are the most straightforward way to do that.

And once you're locked in, the leverage flips. As one hosting comparison site put it bluntly: "Squarespace is a closed platform. Your site lives on their servers, under their terms. If they raise prices again in 12 months, your options are pay or start over. That's the real cost of platform dependency."

Squarespace isn't even the worst offender — it's just the one that made headlines this month. Wix raised one plan by about 8% from 2025 to 2026. Shopify has held steady so far, but the broader trajectory across website builders and SaaS platforms is unmistakably upward. And the increases are compounding. One Reddit user tracked the full arc: from 2015 to 2022, prices rose 15% over seven years. Then 2023–2024 brought a 20% jump. 2025–2026 added another 12%. And the coming year was shaping up to be the biggest single-year hike yet: 28%.

The pattern is the same whether you're on Squarespace, Wix, or a dozen SaaS tools stacked on top of each other. You signed up for a price. You got invested — built your site, configured your workflows, trained your team. Then the price went up, and the new features you were allegedly paying for were things you'd never asked for and couldn't opt out of.

## The true cost of "renting"

Here's where it gets genuinely expensive.

The advertised subscription price is not what you pay. Multiple independent analyses in 2026 converged on the same finding: the true total cost of ownership (TCO) for SaaS is consistently **2.5 to 4 times the headline subscription price**. That $24,000-a-year tool actually costs $60,000 to $96,000 once you account for implementation, integrations, per-seat scaling, customization fees, and the annual price increases baked into the contract.

Then there's the waste. Zylo's 2026 SaaS Management Index found that **46% of SaaS licenses go unused each month** — representing nearly $19.8 million in wasted spend per organization annually. The average mid-size business now runs 130 SaaS applications. You're not just paying for software you don't use; you're paying for software your team has never even logged into.

And the AI surcharge is its own line item now. AI-feature surcharges have driven SaaS expenses up by an average of **34% since 2024** — costs that vendors add to your bill whether or not you touch the AI features. The Squarespace users who posted angrily on Reddit about paying for AI they didn't want weren't complaining about a one-off. They were early witnesses to a structural shift: SaaS pricing is increasingly built around features the vendor wants to sell, not features the customer needs to buy.

Some CTOs have started calling it the "SaaSpocalypse" — subscription fees outpacing actual utility, with no end in sight because the vendor controls the pricing lever and you control nothing.

## The old escape hatch: "Just build it yourself"

For years, the standard advice for escaping SaaS dependency was to build custom software. The math was straightforward, and in many cases it was correct: a custom solution typically costs $25,000–$65,000 upfront plus $500–$3,000 per month for maintenance, and the breakeven point against SaaS subscriptions could land within 12–24 months. Over five years, the numbers tilt even further toward ownership — no per-seat fees, no annual price hikes, no paying for features you didn't ask for.

The problem was never the math. The problem was the execution.

Building custom software required a development team — or at minimum, a developer you trusted and could afford. It meant scoping requirements, writing specs, waiting months for an MVP, debugging what the developer built versus what you actually meant, and then paying $2,000–$8,000 a month forever after to maintain the thing. For a small business owner running a photography studio, a yoga studio, a consultancy, or a local service, "just build custom software" was advice that assumed resources they didn't have and time they couldn't spare.

So the choice was binary: rent software you don't fully control, at a price that rises every year, paying for features you didn't ask for — or spend tens of thousands of dollars and months of your life building something from scratch, if you could even find the right developer.

That binary is false now. There's a third option.

## When to build, when to buy

Not every piece of software should be built from scratch. The decision isn't ideological; it's mathematical. Here's the framework that 2026 analyses converge on:

**Buy (subscribe) when** the function isn't a competitive differentiator, a proven SaaS solution covers 80%+ of your needs, and your timeline doesn't allow for even a short build. Email, accounting, team chat — these are commodity functions where SaaS still makes sense.

**Build when** your workflow is a competitive advantage, off-the-shelf SaaS meets only about 70% of your needs, you expect 20–50+ users where per-seat costs compound, or compliance requires full data control. This is the zone where the TCO math flips: the 2.5–4x subscription multiplier means a tool that looks like $500/month is actually costing you $1,500–$2,000/month in real terms, and that number only goes up.

**Build with AI** when you're in the "build" zone but don't have a development team, can't afford a $50,000 upfront investment, and can't wait six months for an MVP. This is the option that didn't exist until 2026 — and it's the one that changes the equation for every small business owner who has been trapped between renting forever and a build cost they couldn't justify.
