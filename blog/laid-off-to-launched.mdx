## The wave you're part of

You are not an outlier. You are a data point in the largest AI-attributed layoff cycle in tech history, and the companies doing the cutting aren't shy about saying so.

Goldman Sachs estimates that AI-attributed payroll reductions across major U.S. employers are running at more than 16,000 per month in 2026. The running list from TechCrunch reads like an industry roll call:

- **[Monday.com](https://monday.com "Monday.com work management platform")** announced plans to reduce around 20% of its workforce, describing the move as part of an "AI-driven growth strategy."
- **Block** cut 4,000 jobs — 40% of its workforce — with CEO Jack Dorsey explicitly attributing the decision to AI, stating that "intelligence tools have changed what it means to build and run a company."
- **Intuit** eliminated roughly 3,000 jobs (17% of its workforce) in a restructuring centered on AI.
- **Oracle** reduced its workforce by 21,000 employees over the past year, attributing the cuts to increased AI investment.
- **Cloudflare** cut over 1,000 jobs. CEO Matthew Prince didn't just cite AI — he published a Wall Street Journal opinion piece arguing that AI had made those roles obsolete, and warned of more cuts as the company shifts toward "an agentic AI era."
- **Snap** cut 1,000 positions, with CEO Evan Spiegel telling employees that AI advancements were "reducing repetitive work."

The pattern is unmistakable. These companies aren't shrinking because business is bad. Most of them are profitable. They're shrinking because AI made enough of their workforce redundant that the math changed. As Cloudflare's CEO put it: the roles aren't coming back.

So the question isn't whether AI will change your industry. It already did — that's why you're here. The question is what you do with the next ninety days.

## The story underneath the headlines

Here's the part the layoff trackers miss.

Between November 2025 and January 2026, Americans filed **1.56 million new business applications** — the largest three-month stretch on record since the Census Bureau started tracking in 2004. January alone saw 532,319 filings, 36.8% above the same month a year earlier.

As Forbes' Lisa Curtis pointed out, when a laid-off product marketer starts a consulting firm with an AI research stack and one contractor, that job doesn't appear in any large-company hiring report. It appears in the Census Bureau's EIN filings. The boom is real. It's just invisible to the people writing layoff trend pieces.

The framing from PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer was even more direct: AI isn't just changing jobs, it's changing the risk-reward calculus of building a company. When one operator with AI agents can do what previously required a Series A team, the math for a would-be founder shifts — and so does the decision for a laid-off senior manager weighing whether to keep chasing a director role or ship something of their own.

## Why the old startup playbook is broken

Here's what used to happen when you wanted to start a software business after a layoff. You'd have the idea, maybe even the domain expertise from whatever job just ended. But then you'd hit the wall:

You needed a **technical co-founder**. Good luck. The ones worth their equity already had offers, and the ones available were a coin flip. You'd spend three months on co-founder dating, going to mixers, sliding into DMs, burning through severance while explaining your vision to people who had their own visions.

You needed **startup capital**. Even if you found a developer willing to work for equity, you needed hosting, a database, domain registration, maybe a designer. A lean MVP still meant $5,000–$15,000 before you had a single user. On a severance package with an unclear runway, that felt like gambling.

You needed **time**. Months of it. First the design, then the frontend, then the backend, then the auth system, then the database setup, then the deployment, then the debugging when `localhost` worked but production didn't. By the time you launched, the window had shifted, your confidence had eroded, and you'd been living on savings so long that panic had replaced clarity.

That playbook assumed a fundamental constraint: building software required a team. The constraint is gone.

## The new math: one person, a full stack

In 2026, the solo founder success stories aren't aspirational myths. They're case studies with revenue numbers.

Consider the documented solo-built products that hit six and seven figures: **Photo.ai**, built by one person, generates AI-generated photos and reportedly clears millions in ARR. **PDF.ai** — chat with your PDFs — started as a weekend project by Seth Kramer and scaled on usage-based pricing. **HeadshotPro**, **Cal AI**, **TypingMind** — all one-person operations, all profitable.

The pattern isn't that these founders were geniuses. The pattern is that the tools finally caught up to the ambition. As one analysis put it: a decade ago, building a software company meant a team, a runway, and a year of work. In 2026, one person with an AI model and the right skills can ship a product people pay for in a weekend.

But "the right skills" is where the gap opens up again. Because here's what those success stories have in common that the layoff-to-launch articles conveniently skip: every one of those founders could **code**. They knew how to wire up an auth system, deploy a database, set up a CI pipeline, and debug a production crash at 2 AM. The AI amplified their existing engineering judgment. It didn't replace the need for it.

What about everyone else? The product manager who just got laid off from Intuit. The customer success lead who lost her role at Monday.com. The operations specialist whose position at Cisco vanished. They have industry knowledge, they have a network, they have the domain expertise to know exactly what software their old industry needs — but they can't write a React component, and they certainly can't configure a Prisma migration or set up RBAC from scratch.

## The ninety-day playbook

Here's what I'd do if I got the email next Tuesday.

**Days 1–14: Grieve, then inventory.** Let the shock pass. Then sit down with a notebook and write out every inefficiency, every workaround, every "someone should build a tool for this" moment you experienced in your last role. Those are your product candidates. Pick the one where you can name ten people who'd pay for it tomorrow.

**Days 15–30: Validate.** Not with a landing page — with conversations. Call the ten people. Ask them what they're doing about the problem today. If five of them say "nothing, but it's killing me," you have demand. If they say "we use a spreadsheet," you have a competitor to beat on simplicity.

**Days 31–60: Build.** This is where the old playbook would have you hunting for a developer. Instead, you describe your product to a platform like [AutoCoder](https://www.autocoder.cc/platform?utm_source=blog&utm_medium=latest&utm_campaign=LaidOffToLaunched "Describe your idea and AutoCoder generates the app"): the screens, the user flows, the data model, the roles. You don't write code. You describe the business, and the platform generates the application — auth, database, API, frontend, all of it. Then you iterate. The loop is tight: you describe a change, it builds, you test, you adjust. In a week, you have a working app. In a month, you have a product.

**Days 61–90: Launch.** Go back to those ten people. Give them access. Listen to what breaks, what confuses them, what they wish existed. Fix it — in conversation, not in a sprint planning meeting. Then ask them to pay. The first $100 is the hardest. After that, you're not a laid-off worker with an idea. You're a founder with revenue.
