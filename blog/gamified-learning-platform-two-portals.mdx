## A Learning Experience Students Actually Want to Open

<Frame caption="One codebase, two experiences: a playful student portal on the left, a protected teacher analytics portal on the right">
  ![Gamified learning platform built with AutoCoder showing a brick-themed student portal with quizzes, XP, and a leaderboard beside a teacher portal with class overview, skill mastery heatmap, and frequent mistakes](/images/blog/gamified-learning-platform-two-portals/01.png "Gamified learning platform with student and teacher portals")

What if an online learning platform felt less like homework—and more like the next level of a game?

That is the idea behind this **gamified learning platform built with [AutoCoder](https://www.autocoder.cc/platform?utm_source=blog&utm_medium=stories&utm_campaign=GamifiedLearning "Build full-stack apps with AutoCoder.cc")**: a colorful, brick-inspired student experience for answering questions, unlocking knowledge stages, tracking progress, and climbing a leaderboard—paired with a completely separate teacher portal for understanding how students learn.

The compelling part is not only what the product does. It is how quickly a fairly sophisticated education product can take shape inside AutoCoder.

From one project, AutoCoder created two role-specific experiences:

- A lightweight **student portal** focused on play, momentum, and visible achievement.
- A protected **teacher dashboard** focused on learning quality, frequent mistakes, weak knowledge areas, and classroom decisions.

One shared codebase. Two clearly separated journeys. A much shorter path from product idea to working application.

> **Project snapshot:** Explore the original [AutoCoder education project](https://www.autocoder.cc/en/projects/1742166031?PROJECTID=PROJ_67d7580f "Open the AutoCoder gamified education project").

The real education problem is not access — it is momentum.

Most students do not need another static list of assignments. They need a reason to begin, a clear signal that they are improving, and a next step that feels achievable.

At the same time, educators rarely need "more data" in the abstract. They need answers to practical questions:

- Which concepts create the most mistakes?
- Is a student guessing, improving, or quietly falling behind?
- Which topic should be reviewed next?
- Is the current difficulty level helping—or blocking—progress?

This product treats those needs as two sides of the same learning loop.

```
Student plays → the system captures learning signals → the teacher sees patterns
     ↑                                                              ↓
     └────────────── teaching adapts and the next challenge improves ┘
```

The result is more than an online quiz. It is a feedback system designed around motivation and action.

## The student portal: turn "practice" into "one more level"

The student home page removes friction. Instead of opening on a dense course catalog, it brings the learner directly to fast, playable questions.

<Frame caption="The student home screen opens straight into playable questions, with XP, level progress, and the leaderboard always in view">
  ![Brick-themed student quiz screen showing four math questions with instant correct-answer feedback, an XP counter at 1,250, a level 7 progress bar, and a class leaderboard](/images/blog/gamified-learning-platform-two-portals/02.png "Student quiz screen with XP and leaderboard")
</Frame>

*A playful student experience turns quick practice, instant feedback, XP, and friendly competition into one continuous motivation loop.*

### 1. Quick quizzes create an instant win

Students can begin answering immediately. Short question cycles make practice feel lightweight, while points and visible feedback turn each response into a moment of progress.

The interaction is intentionally simple:

1. Read the question.
2. Choose an answer.
3. Receive immediate feedback.
4. Earn progress and continue.

This is the educational equivalent of a great product onboarding flow: value arrives before complexity.

### 2. The leaderboard makes effort visible

A leaderboard adds friendly social energy to the experience. Scores are no longer hidden in a private gradebook; achievement becomes visible, legible, and worth returning for.

Used thoughtfully, rankings can celebrate consistency rather than punish mistakes. The design can reward accumulated XP, completed levels, streaks, or improvement—giving every learner more than one way to succeed.

### 3. Level Map turns knowledge into a journey

The **Level Map** is the visual heart of the student experience. Different knowledge areas become stages in a larger adventure. A learner does not simply "complete fractions." They clear a stage, see the next destination, and understand how today's work connects to a broader path.

This map solves three common problems at once:

- It makes curriculum structure understandable at a glance.
- It creates anticipation for what comes next.
- It transforms completion into a visible collection of achievements.

The brick-inspired art direction reinforces the metaphor beautifully: knowledge is something students build, piece by piece.

### 4. My Progress makes growth concrete

The **My Progress** area translates answer history into visual feedback. Instead of expecting students to interpret rows of numbers, it presents performance as charts, completion states, trends, achievements, and areas to strengthen.

Good progress visualization answers three questions in seconds:

- Where am I now?
- How far have I come?
- What should I do next?

That clarity helps students develop ownership of learning without exposing the complexity of the educator's analytics tools.

## The teacher portal: turn activity into teaching intelligence

The student side is playful by design. The teacher side is analytical by design.

AutoCoder generated an independent educator workspace where the information architecture, permissions, and actions match a completely different job: diagnosing learning and deciding what to change.

<Frame caption="The educator workspace trades play for diagnosis: score distribution, skill mastery, answer quality, and the question bank in one view">
  ![Teacher Portal dashboard showing class overview with 28 students and 74% average score, a score distribution chart, a skill mastery heatmap, frequent mistakes ranked by percentage of students, answer quality gauges, and a question bank panel](/images/blog/gamified-learning-platform-two-portals/04.png "Teacher Portal analytics dashboard")
</Frame>

*Student activity becomes teaching intelligence: educators can move from a class-wide pattern to a targeted intervention without leaving the platform.*

### 1. Frequent mistakes reveal where teaching should focus

A high-frequency error view surfaces the questions and concepts students miss repeatedly. This is more useful than an average score alone. A class can appear healthy overall while still sharing one important misconception.

By grouping repeated errors, teachers can move from vague concern to targeted action:

- Re-teach a specific concept.
- Assign a focused practice set.
- Adjust the wording of a confusing question.
- Compare individual and class-wide patterns.

### 2. Content and difficulty remain under educator control

The backend is not only a reporting surface. Teachers can manage the question bank, edit exercises, configure levels, control difficulty, and organize tasks.

That closes the loop between insight and intervention. When the dashboard reveals a weak area, the educator can respond inside the same product instead of exporting data into a disconnected workflow.

## Tutorial: how I built this product in AutoCoder, step by step

The finished product looks like a coordinated system, but I did not try to perfect every screen in one enormous prompt. I built it in layers: define the architecture, generate the first version, test the critical paths, and then refine one problem at a time.

Here is the exact workflow I used—and a version you can adapt for your own education product.

### Step 1: define the two users before describing the pages

Before opening AutoCoder, I reduced the idea to a simple role matrix:

| Role | Primary goal | Can access | Must not access |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Student | Learn, answer, progress | Quizzes, Level Map, leaderboard, personal progress | Question editing, class analytics, teacher settings |
| Educator | Diagnose and improve learning | Dashboard, mistake analysis, heatmaps, question bank, difficulty and level controls | Student-only game flow as an authenticated learner |

This prevented a common prompting mistake: listing many features without explaining who owns each one.

**What I wanted AutoCoder to understand:** this was not one dashboard with two menu states. It was one product foundation with two deliberately isolated experiences.

### Step 2: send one architecture-first prompt

My first prompt described the complete product loop, the permission model, and the minimum demonstrable content. The essential version looked like this:

```
Build a web-based dual-role sandbox learning platform for education.

Create two completely separate portals from one project:

1. Student portal
- Quick online quizzes
- Gamified level progression
- A Level Map that turns knowledge topics into stages
- A leaderboard
- A My Progress page with scores, completion, achievements,
  activity history, and weak-topic guidance
- A lightweight interface suitable for young learners

2. Educator portal
- A dashboard for frequent mistakes and weak knowledge points
- Practice-time statistics and student ability heatmaps
- Answer-quality and class-performance analysis
- Question-bank import and editing
- Difficulty controls, level configuration, and task management

Use one shared codebase but generate separate student and educator
entry points. Isolate routes and permissions so a student cannot reach
teacher pages by changing the URL. Include a sample math learning game.
Use a frontend-first implementation with simple local or mock data so
the complete experience can be previewed immediately.

The final visible product should be in English.
```

Notice what this prompt does beyond naming features:

- It defines the users and their jobs.
- It explains which data must connect the two portals.
- It states the security invariant: students cannot enter educator routes directly.
- It supplies a demo domain—math—so AutoCoder has concrete content to generate.
- It prioritizes a runnable preview instead of waiting for production infrastructure.

**AutoCoder output:** an initial project plan and a staged build path covering the draft, requirement list, and complete project.

### Step 3: use the Draft stage to judge structure, not polish

AutoCoder first generated a draft focused on the homepage style and layout. At this stage, I checked whether the product had the correct bones:

<Frame caption="AutoCoder's three-stage build path: Draft for style and layout, Requirements for specifications and logic, then the complete project">
  ![AutoCoder panel describing three build stages — Draft at about two minutes for style and layout, Requirements at about five minutes for specifications and logic, and Complete project at about fifteen minutes for backend and database](/images/blog/gamified-learning-platform-two-portals/05.png "AutoCoder three-stage build path")
</Frame>

- Was the student's next action immediately obvious?
- Did student and teacher navigation feel like two different products?
- Were quizzes, Level Map, My Progress, and the teacher dashboard represented?
- Was the learning loop understandable before adding more visual detail?

I did not spend time polishing colors yet. A beautiful interface with the wrong role model would have been expensive to unwind later.

**Checkpoint:** do not move forward because the first screen looks impressive. Move forward when the information architecture matches the real user journey.

### Step 4: review the Requirement List as a product specification

Next, I let AutoCoder expand the concept into a requirement list. This was where the idea became testable behavior rather than a collection of screens.

I reviewed the requirements against five questions:

1. What happens after a student submits an answer?
2. How is progress saved and visualized?
3. Which student signals appear in the teacher dashboard?
4. Which controls can change future practice?
5. What happens when the wrong role requests a protected route?

I also made sure the project included realistic states: completed and locked levels, correct and incorrect answers, weak-topic indicators, empty states, and enough sample data for charts to communicate something meaningful.

**Why this step matters:** UI generation is easy to admire, but requirements reveal whether the product will actually behave like a system.

### Step 5: generate the complete project and test the two entry points

After the requirements were aligned, I asked AutoCoder to build the complete project. It generated a working preview with role-specific access and sample accounts for student, educator, and administrator testing.

I tested the product in this order:

1. Open the student entry point and sign in with a student test account.
2. Answer both correct and incorrect questions.
3. Confirm that score, progress, and activity change.
4. Open Level Map and verify locked, current, and completed stages.
5. Open My Progress and check whether the previous actions appear.
6. Sign out and enter through the educator portal.
7. Confirm that frequent mistakes, mastery, and quality views reflect the learning model.
8. Try to open an educator URL while authenticated as a student.

The eighth test is critical. Hiding a teacher navigation item is not permission isolation; blocking the route is.

**AutoCoder output:** a demonstrable end-to-end loop in which student actions produce signals that the educator experience can interpret.

*This student screen was not treated as the finish line. I used it to test whether answering, feedback, scoring, and progression behaved as one continuous flow.*

*I then tested the same data from the educator side to confirm that the two generated portals formed one product loop.*

### Step 6: fix the real navigation failure with a narrow prompt

During testing, I found that the student homepage could fail when the URL did not include a level parameter. Instead of rewriting the whole navigation flow, I used the Visual Edits tool:

<Frame caption="Visual Edits scopes a prompt to one element — here the My Progress section — instead of the whole navigation flow">
  ![AutoCoder Visual Edits interface with a My Progress div selected and highlighted in green, a red arrow pointing to the Visual edits button in the prompt bar](/images/blog/gamified-learning-platform-two-portals/06.png "AutoCoder Visual Edits tool")
</Frame>

Simply place your cursor over the section you want to modify, then enter your prompt.

```
Fix the issue that prevents the student homepage from opening.
Keep all existing permission boundaries and learning logic unchanged.
```

<Frame caption="AutoCoder reports the fix and states explicitly that student permissions were not broadened">
  ![AutoCoder response confirming the student homepage opening issue is fixed, explaining that visiting Home without a level parameter now auto-selects an accessible published level, that levelId deep links still work, and that the fix does not broaden student permissions](/images/blog/gamified-learning-platform-two-portals/07.png "AutoCoder navigation fix confirmation")
</Frame>

AutoCoder repaired the default learner entry so that opening `/` could select an available published level automatically, while links containing a specific `levelId` continued to open the requested level.

This is a useful pattern for AI-assisted development: describe the observable failure, name what must remain unchanged, and let the agent trace the implementation.

**Verification after the fix:**

- Open the default student URL without parameters.
- Open a deep link with a valid level ID.
- Confirm that an invalid or missing parameter does not create a blank page.
- Re-test the student-to-teacher route boundary.

### Step 7: change the student visual system without changing the logic

Once the core flow worked, I changed the emotional character of the student experience. My prompt was intentionally explicit about preserving functionality:

```
Keep all existing functionality and interaction logic unchanged.
Replace the decorative styling of the student-facing frontend with
a colorful interlocking-brick theme.
```

<Frame caption="AutoCoder pauses to confirm scope before restyling — whole project, or student-facing frontend only">
  ![AutoCoder conversation where a restyle request is answered with a scope question asking whether the theme applies to all frontend and backend pages or only student-facing frontend pages, answered with student-facing frontend pages](/images/blog/gamified-learning-platform-two-portals/08.png "AutoCoder scope confirmation before restyle")
</Frame>

AutoCoder asked whether the theme should apply to the whole project or only part of it. I narrowed the scope to the **student-facing frontend only**.

That clarification protected the teacher dashboard from becoming visually noisy. Students received bright colors, brick-like cards, playful decorations, and a more game-like atmosphere; educators kept a cleaner analytical environment.

This iteration updated the shared student shell—including navigation, page backgrounds, login, registration, Level Map, and My Progress—while retaining the existing quiz, filtering, achievement, pagination, and data behavior.

*The visual redesign came after the workflow worked. This sequencing let me compare the new presentation against unchanged progress behavior.*

**Lesson:** when restyling a generated application, always state both the scope and the invariants. "Make it playful" is weaker than "restyle the student frontend only; preserve all logic, routes, state, and data interactions."

### Step 8: run a final role-by-role acceptance test

Before treating the project as complete, I repeated the entire experience as both users.

**Student acceptance checklist**

- The default homepage opens without a special URL parameter.
- Quiz answers return clear feedback.
- XP, scores, or completion update consistently.
- The leaderboard is readable.
- Level Map communicates locked, current, and completed stages.
- My Progress contains English-only visible copy and no empty decoration.
- No educator page is reachable through direct URL entry.

**Educator acceptance checklist**

- Class overview data is legible and internally consistent.
- Frequent mistakes identify useful patterns rather than isolated noise.
- Skill mastery and answer quality are scannable.
- Question-bank, difficulty, task, and level controls are discoverable.
- Student-facing style changes have not damaged the analytical interface.

**Cross-role acceptance checklist**

- Student activity and teacher insight describe the same learning model.
- Signing out clears the previous role context.
- Shared data does not imply shared permissions.
- Both entry points can be demonstrated independently.

## Why AutoCoder is the force multiplier

The standout lesson from this case is not that AI can draw a dashboard. It is that AutoCoder can carry a product idea across multiple layers of application development.

### 1. It turns natural-language intent into product structure

The original brief described roles, user journeys, dashboards, permissions, and a sample math game. AutoCoder translated that intent into routes, page groups, data relationships, and working interface logic.

### 2. It supports iterative visual direction without discarding functionality

The student experience was later restyled with a playful brick theme while keeping its quiz logic, progress states, routes, and data interactions intact. That is an important kind of iteration: changing the emotional character of a product without rebuilding its foundation.

### 3. It makes complex prototypes demonstrable

A dual-role system is difficult to explain with static mockups. A working prototype lets stakeholders experience the difference between the student and teacher journeys, test navigation, review data displays, and spot missing states much earlier.
