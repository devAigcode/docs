A focused **competitor analysis** helps an MVP team make a product decision, not assemble a catalogue of market features. The practical question is: how does a defined user handle this problem today, where do current options create compromises, and what is the smallest alternative workflow worth testing? The output should be a decision-ready scope, with explicit assumptions and exclusions, rather than a long feature matrix.

This method treats competition broadly. A comparable option may be a specialist product, general-purpose software, a spreadsheet, an internal process, an agency, or the choice to tolerate the problem. Reviewing those alternatives on equal terms gives founders, product managers, and designers a more realistic basis for MVP validation and product positioning.

## Set a focused competitor analysis question

**Begin with the product decision the research must support.** A clear question prevents competitive research from becoming broad market browsing and keeps the work proportionate to an early-stage MVP.

Before starting, prepare a short problem framing. It should identify the target user or buyer, the job they are trying to complete, the trigger that starts the job, the desired outcome, and the decision that remains unresolved. This is the main prerequisite for useful research: without it, it is difficult to know which products or workflows are comparable.

For example, “Who are our competitors?” is too broad to guide scope. A more useful question is: “How do operations coordinators collect approval for recurring requests, and what must an MVP enable for them to complete that process with less follow-up?” This wording directs attention to a user, a job, and an observable workflow.

Next, set the comparison boundary. Direct competitors serve a similar audience with a similar approach. Adjacent competitors may cover one stage of the same job. Alternatives include manual methods, service providers, templates, email, or general-purpose tools that users have adapted to their process. The manual option matters because it often explains why a user has not adopted specialist software.

Write down the assumptions that the research should challenge. These may include which segment experiences the problem most often, which step causes the most delay, or whether a proposed benefit is important enough to change behaviour. Naming assumptions does not make the work less objective; it distinguishes what the team believes from what it has observed.

Finally, limit the research set. For an MVP, a small number of well-documented direct options and alternatives is usually more useful than a long, uneven list. Set a time boundary as well. The goal is not a permanent market record, but enough evidence to choose a first workflow to test.

## Collect comparable evidence for competitive analysis

**Use the same research template for every option.** Consistency makes differences meaningful and exposes where the evidence is incomplete. Comparing one product from a homepage and another from detailed documentation can create misleading conclusions.

Start by recording the product promise. Note the audience addressed, the problem described, and the outcome emphasized on public product pages, onboarding material, and help content. These sources may express product positioning differently, so capture the source alongside your summary. Marketing language is not proof that a result occurs, but it is useful evidence of the audience and category the company intends to serve.

Then trace the core workflow. Follow the shortest credible path from the moment a user recognizes the need to the point where they receive a usable outcome. For each option, document:

1. the trigger or entry point;
2. information the user must provide;
3. the central action they take;
4. reviews, approvals, or handoffs; and
5. how the outcome is confirmed or used next.

Apply this structure to manual alternatives too. A spreadsheet-based process may involve gathering information through messages, updating rows, sharing access, following up with reviewers, and reporting status separately. That is a workflow, even if it has no formal onboarding or product interface.

Look beyond visible features to the conditions that make the workflow function. A seemingly simple capability may require imported data, roles, permissions, templates, integration setup, or an administrator. These supporting conditions are part of the user experience and often reveal a trade-off that a feature checklist misses.

Public documentation, tutorials, release notes, community discussions, and review content can add context. Use them carefully. They may reveal a product behaviour or a reported experience, but they are not a representative measure of customer opinion. Label each note as an observed fact, a reported view, or a team inference.

A simple evidence table is sufficient: option, source, date checked, observation, confidence, and implication for the MVP. For example, “The help material describes permission levels” is an observation. “Early users will not need permissions” is an assumption requiring validation. This distinction keeps competitive research from becoming unsupported product requirements.

## Compare workflows instead of collecting features

<Frame caption="Comparing identical job stages across software and manual alternatives reveals trade-offs that feature matrices conceal.">
  ![Workflow comparison diagram of a direct product, manual alternative, and proposed MVP path](/images/blog/how-to-conduct-competitor-analysis-for-a-product-mvp/inline-1.png)
</Frame>

**Map the same job across alternatives to identify relevant friction and trade-offs.** Feature matrices are easy to produce, but they rarely show when a feature matters, how much setup it requires, or whether it helps a user finish the job.

Build a side-by-side journey with one row for each stage of the target job and one column for every competitor or alternative. At each stage, ask:

1. What is the user trying to achieve?
2. What action, information, or decision is required?
3. Where can delay, uncertainty, duplication, or a handoff occur?
4. What does this option make easier, and what does it make harder?

Make trade-offs explicit rather than treating them as failures. A broad platform may support many connected jobs while requiring more configuration. A narrow tool may be quick to adopt but leave collaboration or reporting outside the workflow. A manual process may feel flexible and familiar while making ownership or status difficult to see. These differences provide a more useful basis for product competitor analysis than simple feature presence.

Look for repeated patterns. Useful signals include users re-entering information, waiting for another person, switching between tools, struggling to understand a status, or losing control of a critical decision. Also note complexity that is purposeful: governance, scale, specialist review, or permission controls may be necessary for some users rather than evidence of poor design.

At this point, separate a gap from a differentiator. A gap is something an existing option does not provide. A differentiator is a proposed advantage for a specific user completing a specific job, with a credible way to test whether it matters. “No competitor offers this feature” is not enough. A stronger statement is: “For this user at this stage, a shorter review path may reduce a documented handoff; the MVP will test whether users prefer and understand it.”

Competitive research has limits. Public material can be incomplete, trials may not expose every configuration, and feedback may reflect a particular context. It should inform interviews, workflow observation, and prototype testing, not replace them.

## Turn findings into testable MVP requirements

<Frame caption="A disciplined MVP scope moves from a specific user and job through a limited workflow and exclusions to a planned validation method.">
  ![MVP requirements workflow from user job to validation session](/images/blog/how-to-conduct-competitor-analysis-for-a-product-mvp/inline-2.png)
</Frame>

**Choose one differentiated path and include only the support needed to test it.** An MVP is not a reduced copy of every competitor. It is a practical experiment through which a defined user can attempt a valuable task and the team can learn whether its approach has merit.

Use this sequence to translate competitive research into scope:

1. **Choose one target user and job.** Avoid combining audiences simply because they use related products. If a requester and an approver have different needs, decide whose success the first path is optimizing.

2. **Write a testable value hypothesis.** State the user, situation, proposed workflow, and expected outcome without presenting it as a fact. For instance: “When preparing a recurring request, a coordinator can submit the required information and see approval status without managing the process across separate messages.”

3. **Define the happy path.** List the minimum screens or states needed to begin, supply inputs, take the core action, and receive a usable result. Include the smallest necessary data structure, account handling, roles, and notifications.

4. **Document deliberate exclusions.** Defer adjacent workflows, advanced settings, broad integrations, and reporting that do not test the main hypothesis. An exclusion list protects the learning goal when new feature requests appear.

5. **Add essential trust and recovery conditions.** Consider incomplete work, invalid input, permission boundaries, confirmations, and a clear next step. A prototype does not need every edge case, but users should understand what happened and how to continue.

6. **Choose the validation method before implementation.** Decide what prospective users will attempt, what the team will observe, and what evidence would change the scope. A usability session can test comprehension and workflow fit; a functional pilot can reveal whether the path works in a real process.

## Review the scope before building

**Check that the proposed MVP tests a workflow distinction rather than mere category presence.** A final review can prevent an otherwise disciplined competitive analysis from turning into an unfocused build.

Read the scope against the original research question. Can a colleague identify the target user, trigger, and desired outcome? Does the primary path address a documented trade-off in current options? Are facts separate from assumptions and planned tests? Does each supporting capability help the user complete the selected task or help the team learn from the test?

If these answers are unclear, return to the workflow map rather than adding more features. The underlying problem is often an overly broad job, an undefined user segment, or an untested assumption about why someone would change their current behaviour. Narrowing the path can produce a more credible experiment.

## FAQ

### What is competitor analysis?

Competitor analysis is a structured review of products, services, and alternatives that address a similar user problem. For an MVP, it compares positioning, workflows, and trade-offs to support a specific product decision rather than simply produce a feature list.

### How many competitors should an MVP competitor analysis include?

Include enough direct competitors and alternatives to understand the main approaches to the target job. A small, consistently researched set is generally more useful at the MVP stage than a large list reviewed at different levels of detail.

### What should I look for in a competitor’s product?

Review the target audience, stated promise, core workflow, required setup, roles, supporting conditions, and visible trade-offs. Record the source for each point and keep direct observations separate from assumptions about user needs.

### How can competitor analysis inform product requirements?

It can identify a workflow worth testing, the minimum conditions needed to support it, and capabilities that should be deliberately excluded. The resulting requirements should then be tested with prospective users rather than treated as final conclusions from competitive research.

## Conclusion

Effective **competitor analysis** for a product MVP turns competitive research into a narrow, testable product decision. Define the user and job, collect comparable evidence, map workflows and trade-offs, and scope one path with clear exclusions. The result is not a broader feature inventory; it is a more disciplined basis for MVP validation and product positioning.
